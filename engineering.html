Building Kindrdfood

A video-first telehealth nutrition platform — live consultations, clinical charting, payments, content, and audit. As technical cofounder I was the sole engineer, designing and building it on Ruby on Rails over roughly three years. Here's what's inside and why it's built the way it is.

43 Database tables across a genuine clinical + scheduling domain — not CRUD scaffolding
233 Migrations over ~3 years of continuous schema evolution
~40 ActiveRecord models · 41 controllers · ~9,400 lines of Ruby
3 User types it served — physicians, dietitians, and families — plus internal staff, across two auth realms
3 Billing models on Stripe — pay-per-visit, prepaid packages, recurring subscriptions
1 Sole engineer — technical cofounder, data model through AWS deployment

Tech stack

A pragmatic 2014-era Rails stack, chosen for breadth of integration: live video, payments, background pipelines, image processing, and a customized CMS all in one codebase.

The hard parts

Five subsystems carry the real engineering signal — each is bespoke domain logic, not a library call.

Engine 01 · Scheduling

Overlapping slots with cascading conflict resolution

A generator turns each dietitian's availability windows into overlapping 30- and 60-minute bookable slots, with buffer handling. The hard part is keeping them consistent: booking a 60-minute visit cancels the two overlapping 30-minute options, and the reverse re-opens them — a cascading conflict resolution that runs every time availability or a booking changes.

Engine 02 · Survey carry-forward

A lifecycle questionnaire engine that threads answers

Distinct versioned questionnaires span the appointment lifecycle — pre-appointment intake, session notes, final assessment. Answers carry forward: a later form pre-populates from earlier ones, so the dietitian never re-asks what the family already told us, and the clinical record stays coherent across the visit.

Engine 03 · Growth charts

Pediatric percentiles and z-scores

Height and weight entries are charted against reference data to produce percentiles and z-scores, plus derived nutritional requirements — the kind of clinical computation a pediatric nutrition product actually needs, stored and versioned per member.

Engine 04 · Recipe allergen review

Ingredient-level allergen tagging + multi-stage QA

The in-house chef authors a recipe; dietitians then edit it and tag it at the ingredient level for allergens, which derives the patient groups it's safe for. Before a family ever sees it, the recipe moves through a multi-stage review pipeline where dietitians sign off — so allergen safety is enforced by workflow, not by hoping someone got it right.

Engine 05 · Audit & versioning

IP-tracked, field-level history with visual diffs

A custom version model captures who changed what, when, and from which IP address — with field-level diffs surfaced through a bespoke admin diff-and-rollback UI. The kind of compliance-minded audit trail a HIPAA-adjacent product needs, and few products this size ever attempt.

Architecture notes

  • Presenter / decorator layer (SimpleDelegator-based) keeps display logic — age formatting, metric conversion, full names — off the ActiveRecord models.
  • Dual-identity auth. Separate User and Dietitian Devise resources with namespaced controllers and role-aware post-sign-in routing.
  • Self-rescheduling background workers. Sidekiq jobs chain their own follow-ups — confirmation, then 1-day, then 1-week reminders, with lead-time-aware cascading.
  • Environment-aware uploaders. Local files in development, S3 in production, four generated image versions, and interactive cropping.
  • Polymorphic domain modeling. purchasable, surveyable, and imageable associations, a self-referential role-based user graph, and many-to-many family/patient-group relationships — with nearly every foreign key and lookup column indexed.

A visit, end to end

What the system orchestrates for a single appointment:

  1. Household onboarding

    A multi-stage registration wizard (a registration_stage state machine) builds the family and each member's clinical profile.

  2. Book & pay

    The slot engine offers conflict-free times; checkout runs through Stripe with saved cards, packages, subscriptions, or coupons.

  3. Intake survey

    A pre-appointment questionnaire captures the visit's focus and threads its answers forward into the clinical record.

  4. Live video visit

    An OpenTok session is provisioned per appointment with role-scoped tokens — the dietitian joins as moderator.

  5. Assessment & plan

    The dietitian writes session notes and an assessment, and attaches recommended articles and dietitian-reviewed recipes.

  6. Audit & reminders

    Every change is versioned with actor + IP; background workers schedule the follow-up reminder cascade.

What I'd own in an interview

Stated plainly, because owning the debt reads as senior:

  • The test suite was scaffolding. Generated test files with few real assertions; the riskiest code — billing, scheduling, surveys — was under-tested. First thing I'd add today.
  • Authorization lived in the wrong layer. Pundit policies were stubs; real access control sat in Devise scopes and controller filters. It worked, but it belonged in policies.
  • Fat models, no service layer. Stripe charge logic sat inside the Purchase model; the User model grew past 400 lines. The refactor into services was intended and never done.

What this proves

The impressive thing isn't the framework version — it's that one engineer modeled and shipped a 43-table, multi-role, payment-and-video healthcare product end to end: live video, Stripe billing with coupons and subscriptions, a bespoke scheduling engine, a lifecycle survey engine, pediatric clinical computation, an allergen-review workflow, and compliance-minded audit. Those fundamentals — domain modeling, payments, RBAC, async jobs, video, audit — are exactly what I'd apply today.
